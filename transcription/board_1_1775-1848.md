## Column: Left (introduction; no header card)

### THE COMMUNICATIONS REVOLUTION
(Title printed underlined.)

The communication revolution from 1775 to 2020 transitioned from physical, print-based media to instant, global digital connectivity. It evolved through four major stages: the revolutionary press and postal systems, the 19th-century electric telegraph, early 20th-century mass media (radio/TV), and the internet-driven digital information age

### These Trifolds will cover the following
- **1775–1800s (Print & Post):** The American Revolution was fueled by newspapers, pamphlets (e.g., *Common Sense*), and committees of correspondence, establishing the press as a critical, politically active force.
- **1800s–1900s (The Electric Age):** The telegraph and telephone eliminated the constraint of distance, turning news and business into real-time endeavors and enabling rapid expansion.
- **20th Century (Mass Communication):** Radio and television created a shared culture, allowing information to reach mass audiences instantly.
- **1990s–2020 (The Digital/Information Age):** The internet democratized information, shifting from one-way communication to interactive social media. Communication became, via smart devices, ubiquitous, mobile, and almost instantaneous.

### (Closing statement, untitled)
This transformation diminished the importance of distance, allowing for immediate sharing of information and making global connectivity a standard feature of a modern future

## Column: COMMUNICATIONS / REVOLUTIONARY WAR AND WAR OF 1812 / 1775-1818

### Documents and Publications
- Pamphlets and Broadsides: Distributed widely to inform and persuade the public about revolutionary ideas.
- Common Sense: Thomas Paine's influential pamphlet that argued for independence and rallied support among colonists.
- The Declaration of Independence: A formal statement that articulated the colonies’ grievances and justified their break from Britain.
- Letters and Essays: Writings by influential figures like John Adams and Benjamin Franklin that spread revolutionary sentiments.
- Newspapers: Local and colonial newspapers published articles and editorials that fostered discussion and unity among colonists.
- Committees of Correspondence: Networks that shared information and coordinated resistance efforts across the colonies.

### Bugles and Drums
- Drums and bugles were essential for communication on the battlefield.
- They signaled commands and movements to troops, especially in noisy environments.
- Drummers maintained the rhythm for marching, helping soldiers keep in step.
- Buglers conveyed specific messages, such as calls for assembly or retreat.
- These instruments fostered morale and unity among soldiers during combat.
- They were also used in ceremonies and parades to honor victories and fallen soldiers.

### Smoke Signals
- Used by Native American tribes and some colonial forces for long-distance communication.
- Employed to signal troop movements or alert nearby forces of danger.
- Created distinct patterns, colors and puffs of smoke to convey specific messages or codes.
- Allowed for quick transmission of information across difficult terrain.
- Served as a means to coordinate attacks or retreats without using written messages.
- Helped maintain secrecy and avoid interception by enemy forces.

### Flags
(Printed as "Flags :" with a space before the colon.)
- Flags served as visual signals to convey messages over long distances.
- Different colors and patterns represented specific commands or alerts.
- Signal flags were used to coordinate troop movements and strategies.
- The use of flags allowed for quick communication without the need for written messages.
- Naval forces utilized flags to communicate between ships and with shore commands.
- The system of flag signaling was crucial for maintaining secrecy and surprise in military operations.

### Pigeons
- Pigeons were used as messengers to carry important military communications.
- They could fly long distances and navigate back to their home lofts.
- The Continental Army utilized trained pigeons for quick and reliable information transfer.
- Pigeons helped relay messages during critical battles, enhancing coordination.
- Some pigeons were even awarded medals for their service in the war

### Cryptography
- Cryptography was employed to secure communications among revolutionary leaders.
- Invisible Ink was one method to hide critical messages
- Codes and ciphers helped protect sensitive information from British interception.
- The use of secret messages  facilitated coordination of military strategies.
- Encrypted letters allowed for safe communication between distant troops.
- Revolutionary pamphlets sometimes used coded language to rally support.
- The concept of decentralized communication foreshadowed modern cryptographic practices.

## Column: Beginnings of the Communications Revolution / 1820-1848

### The Birth of the Electric Telegraph
(Title printed in bold.)
- **Concept & Development (1832-1835):** After learning about electromagnetism, Morse conceived an electric telegraph that used electric impulses to move a marker, recording codes on paper.
- **[red]Morse Code[/red] (1838):** Together with partner [red]Alfred Vail[/red], Morse developed a system of dots, dashes, and spaces representing letters and numbers, enabling complex messages to be sent.
- **Technical Refinements:** Vail improved the device by adding weights to the turning key, substituting a stylus for a pencil, and enhancing the recorder.
- **First Official Line & Message (1844):** Morse secured US government funding to build a 40-mile line between Washington D.C. and Baltimore. On May 24, 1844, he sent the first message
- **Application in the Mexican-American War (1848)** First used in military action in the Mexican-American War supporting logistics, and enabling of battlefield updates. This also provided a means to transmit battle progress from Mexico back to the United States as the war progressed.
- **Future Advantages Recognized**: Military Officers recognized the importance of the telegraph and set the stage for it's widespread use in the Civil War.

### Expansion of the Postal Service
- **Postal Reform Act of 1845:** Faced with declining revenue due to cheaper private express competitors, Congress drastically lowered rates (to 5 or 10 cents based on distance) and strengthened the Post Office's monopoly, which increased volume from 24.5 million letters in 1843 to 62 million by 1849.
- **Infrastructure Expansion:** The use of railroads officially recognized as post roads increased in 1838, accelerating in the 1840s to replace horse-drawn stages in the East.
- **Connecting the West:** The department financed steamship routes through the Isthmus of Panama to reach California and utilized stagecoaches from Tennessee and Missouri, crucial for communication during the western settlement boom.
- **Crackdown on Private Firms:** To regain control, the government passed laws in 1845 restricting private express companies from carrying letters on routes where the U.S. Mail was regularly transported.
- **International Reach:** By the mid-1840s, the Post Office began expanding its network to includeinternational treaties and better services for connecting to foreign locations.

### Impact of [red]Steam-Powered Printing[/red] (Technological Shift)
(Title: "Impact of" and "(Technological Shift)" in bold; "Steam-Powered Printing" in red, underlined.)
- **Mass Production Capability:** Steam-driven presses (such as the two-cylinder press) replaced hand-powered presses, raising production from roughly 125 papers per hour to over 18,000 per hour.
- **Continuous Production:** The shift to printing on continuous rolls rather than individual sheets allowed for rapid, continuous printing.
- **Reduced Unit Costs:** Increased speed drastically reduced the cost per paper, making it financially viable to sell papers for only one cent.
- **Resulting Impacts:** Improved communication of news for the public at reasonable cost (ie. Like the Penny Press)
